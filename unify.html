Overview

The Problem

62% of newcomers to Canada struggle to find the resources they need: government sites, Reddit threads, and WhatsApp chats all give different answers. Another 70% struggle to adapt due to cultural and language barriers, often without a community to lean on.

Unify centralizes trusted settlement information, AI guidance, and a newcomer community in one platform through the Unify App and Unify Gather (in person, community events).

Context

Why this mattered

Witnessing firsthand the struggles of newcomers to Canada, the Unify team set out to create a platform to eliminate confusion and isolation that comes with settlement. The app combines a personalized settlement checklist, an AI companion grounded in government sources, short "Learn" lessons, and a community layer of forums, interest groups, and small-group matching ("Unify Circles"). Beyond the app, Unify Gather runs in-person events with our partners, extending that support offline.

My role centered on strategy and partnerships: building the network of organizations that gave Unify reach and credibility, and running the research that shaped what the product needed to do.

Process

How I approached it

01

Problem validation

Ran 32 user interviews with newcomers to confirm where settlement actually breaks down: scattered information, no roadmap, and isolation came up consistently.

02

Competitor & feature analysis

Studied how other newcomer-support platforms structured their "Learn" content and ran UX audits across Learn and the rest of the app, then used the findings to shape Unify's 21 modules and 20 task workflows, as well as the overall UX.

03

Partner outreach

Contacted 136 organizations across settlement services, post-secondary, finance, and community orgs, converting 15 into active partnerships including RBC, EY, SFU, and YMCA BC.

04

Beta testing

Ran 16 live beta tests with real newcomers to validate the modules, workflows, and partner-sourced content before wider rollout.

Key insight

Unify's whole premise is that newcomers can't tell which advice is current or relevant to their situation. An app saying "trust us" doesn't solve that, but an app backed by RBC, SFU, and the Vancouver Public Library does.

That reframed partnerships from a distribution channel into the credibility layer the product needed to work at all.

Decisions

Where judgment calls were made

The question

136 organizations were contacted but only 15 became partners. With a small team and limited time, how do you decide who's worth pursuing?

What I decided

Prioritized organizations with reach, credibility, and existing newcomer programs -> built-in audiences and faster alignment. Later widened the criteria to community organizations more broadly: our workshops applied just as well to youth and anyone learning how Canadian systems work.

The question

Competitor analysis of the "Learn" feature led to changes in how the 21 modules and 20 workflows were structured. What did that analysis actually reveal, and what changed?

What I decided

Across comparable apps, structured lessons were what newcomers actually finished, more than open-ended AI chat or community feeds. That moved Learn from a secondary feature to the most prominent of Unify's three pillars, and shaped the 21 modules and 20 workflows to be short and task-based rather than reference material.

The question

32 interviews surfaced findings relevant to both partnerships and product. How did insights move between the two workstreams?

What I decided

Interview takeaways went into one shared synthesis doc, not two. The recurring pain points, like confusion over which institution handles what, became concrete examples for partner conversations (showing where their programs could plug in) and a starting list of Learn module topics.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

What worked

Research gave direction, but building early was the real unlock. Putting an MVP and rough prototypes in front of newcomers and partners surfaced what mattered faster than more research would. Feedback doesn't promise a perfect product, but it reliably produces a better one.

What I'd change

I'd pull the competitor analysis and a rough Learn prototype earlier, before outreach started. Walking into a conversation with SFU or RBC with something tangible to react to, not just a pitch, would've sped up both the partnership and the build.

What I learned

Outreach at this scale is mostly a numbers game until it isn't: most of the 136 never responded, and the 15 that did weren't won by a perfect pitch but by consistent follow-up. Once rejection and silence felt like part of the ratio, not a verdict on the idea, they stopped being discouraging.
