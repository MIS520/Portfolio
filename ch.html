Overview

The Brief

The brief centered on Mobile Transfers: how customers find, add, and manage the contacts they send money to, and where that experience breaks down. The institution brought in an experiential learning team to research this space and design improvements, working across a distributed stakeholder group spanning research, design, engineering, and compliance.

All of it operated within the institution's disclosure and legal limits on what could be recorded, shared, or shown outside the org.

Context

Why this mattered

Contact Hub was the working name for this engagement: a fixed-term placement, January to April 2026, brought in to research the Mobile Transfers contact experience and hand off recommendations the institution's own teams could act on once it ended.

My role was to lead that research and the design work that followed: scoping what we could realistically investigate, building the frameworks the team used to run and analyze interviews, and keeping documentation usable for people who hadn't been in the room for the original conversations.

Because of the institution's disclosure and legal requirements, its name and any identifying details are withheld here. What follows is the research and design process itself: how the work was scoped, run, and handed off.

Process

How I approached it

01

Scoping the research

Worked with the distributed stakeholder group across research, design, engineering, and compliance to define what the research could and couldn't cover. The institution's disclosure rules shaped that scope as much as the product question did.

02

Building interview & analysis frameworks

Built the frameworks the team used to run interviews and synthesize findings consistently, so results from different stakeholders and sessions could be compared against each other rather than treated as one-off conversations.

03

Facilitating workshops

Ran design, feedback, and stakeholder workshops to surface insights from the research and align the group on which opportunity areas mattered most, tying each one back to a measurable business outcome.

04

Maintaining living documentation

Updated documentation throughout the engagement so findings stayed usable once the team's time was up, since no one would be there afterward to explain it.

Key insight

"Research only does its job if someone can still use it after the team that ran it is gone."

That's the constraint an experiential learning engagement puts on the work. There's a fixed end date, and after it, the people who built the context for every finding are no longer in the room. Documentation wasn't an afterthought; it was part of the deliverable, written for stakeholders who would inherit the findings without having sat in on the interviews.

That shaped how the interview and analysis frameworks were built too: not just to capture what people said, but to capture it in a form the next reader could pick up cold.

Decisions

Where judgment calls were made

The question

The stakeholder group spanned research, design, engineering, and compliance, each with different priorities and vocabulary. How do you build a single interview and analysis framework that works across all of them?

What I decided

My own background spans UX, development, and business, so I used that as the throughline. The framework's core categories stayed the same for everyone, but how findings were written up was tailored: more technical detail for engineering, more narrative framing for design and business stakeholders.

The question

The institution's disclosure and legal limitations restricted what could be documented or shared. How did you decide what could go into the living documentation versus what had to stay verbal or internal?

What I decided

The institution's team was the primary authority on disclosure, so I kept communication with them frequent and direct, checking in before documenting anything in a gray area. Where their guidance didn't cover a specific case, the call was mine, built from what stakeholders had already told me about what felt safe to generalize. In practice, that meant generalizing much of the research to the e-Transfer experience as a whole rather than the contact feature alone, which made the documentation useful to other teams working on related parts of the product too.

The question

Workshops surfaced more opportunity areas than the institution could act on at once. How did you decide which ones made it into the final recommendations to senior stakeholders?

What I decided

Brought the full list back to the team and stakeholders rather than deciding alone. Each opportunity area was weighed against business value, but customer value carried more weight in the final cut, since it matched the institution's own customer-focused priorities and made the recommendations easier for senior stakeholders to act on.

The question

The top-priority opportunity area still needed to become a concrete feature recommendation. What direction did the research point toward, and what changed in the design?

What I decided

The clearest pattern across interviews was how customers add and choose recipients for an e-Transfer: similar names, outdated details, and no clear way to tell a trusted recipient from a new one were recurring sources of hesitation and error. That became the basis for the feature direction: surface verified, frequently-used recipients first, flag likely duplicates when someone adds a new contact, and make a recipient's status clearer before a transfer is confirmed. The journey map above reflects that flow.

The senior engineering team responsible for the product received the recommendations well, noting they were specific and well-grounded enough to act on directly.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

What worked

Setting clear guidelines for each framework early gave the team a consistent way to run interviews and write up findings, and kept communication with stakeholders constant rather than clustered around milestones.

What I'd change

I'd push for earlier, more explicit alignment with the institution on disclosure boundaries, so the documentation framework didn't need revising once those boundaries became clearer partway through.

What I learned

How to frame research for senior stakeholders: leading with customer value, then business value, made recommendations land faster than leading with methodology.
