Context

Why this mattered

Banking on Intelligence was built for IAT 355: New Media Technologies. The brief was open: pick a dataset, build visualizations from it, and use those visualizations to tell a story. The team chose AI in banking, since it's a sector where AI has moved from experimentation to production at scale, and the dataset (Evident Insights' AI maturity index for the top 50 banks) gave us a way to connect a fast-moving technology trend to concrete financial outcomes.

My role centered on content: deciding what story the data could actually support, which visualizations would carry that story, and writing the analysis that ties each chart back to the project's central question.

Process

How I approached it

01

Framing the research question

Before any data was collected, set the project's learning goals: understand how AI affects banks internally, through development and operations, and externally, through customer-facing products and satisfaction.

02

Source identification & data gathering

Worked with Nathan and Tirsten to decide which visualizations would carry the narrative: an industry-wide AI adoption comparison, a history of AI in banking, a bank performance bubble chart, a breakdown of AI use cases by type, and a regional comparison.

Analyzed each visualization once built, pulling out findings such as the Capital One outlier in the performance chart and the regional differences in AI use cases, and wrote them into the page's narrative copy.

Key insight

"The relationship between AI maturity and profitability isn't one-directional. It's a reinforcing cycle."

That framing came out of the bank performance bubble chart, where the top-ranked AI banks led in both AI maturity and ROE, but a single outlier (Capital One) complicated the pattern for mid-tier banks. Rather than treat that outlier as noise, the copy explains why it's there: Capital One's card-focused model carries structurally higher ROE, separate from its AI maturity rank. Once that's accounted for, the broader pattern holds.

That shaped how the surrounding text for every visualization was written: not just describing what a chart shows, but explaining what would otherwise look like a contradiction.

Decisions

Where judgment calls were made

The question

AI adoption data spans 16 industries, but the project's focus is banking specifically. How was the decision made to include an industry-wide comparison at all, and where does it sit in the narrative?

What I decided

Included for narrative reasons. Showing AI adoption across all 16 industries first establishes how widespread AI already is, so the banking-specific findings later on read as part of that broader pattern rather than an isolated case. It's the first visualization on the page, setting up why a deep dive into one industry was worth doing.

The question

The bank performance bubble chart shows Capital One as a clear outlier that could distort the headline pattern between AI maturity and ROE. How was that handled in the analysis copy?

What I decided

Named it directly rather than smoothing it into the trend line. Capital One's card-focused model gives it structurally higher ROE that has little to do with AI maturity, and a reader who noticed that outlier without an explanation would have doubted the whole chart. Calling it out by name cost a sentence but kept the broader pattern credible.

The question

Findings were drawn from four sources (Evident Insights, Yahoo Finance, the Wall Street Journal, and Forbes), each with different scopes and update cadences. How were these reconciled into one coherent set of claims?

What I decided

Evident Insights' index set the list of 50 banks and their AI maturity rankings. Yahoo Finance and the Wall Street Journal supplied the financial metrics for those same banks, and Forbes filled in historical context the other two didn't cover. Where a bank's figures didn't line up across sources, or a bank in the index had no clean match in the financial data, it was footnoted or left out rather than forced to fit.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

What worked

Settling on the five visualizations before writing any copy. It meant the narrative was built around what the data could actually show, not retrofitted to an idea we'd already decided to tell.

What I'd change

Cross-reference the financial data against Evident's bank list earlier. A few banks in the AI index didn't have clean matches in Yahoo Finance or the WSJ, and catching that sooner would have saved some reshuffling of the performance chart later on.

What I learned

How much editorial judgment sits inside "data-driven" storytelling. The numbers didn't speak for themselves: deciding what to foreground, what to footnote, and what to cut shaped the story as much as the data did.
