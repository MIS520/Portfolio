From zero to 2nd place: my season as a regionals presenter

In December, I put my name in for something I'd never done before. By March, I was on stage at Western Canada Regionals, and our team placed 2nd: part of the club's best regional result in nearly a decade. Here's what happened in the months between.

Michelle So. · March 2026 · ~5 min read

On stage at Western Canada Regionals

Putting my name in

Each year, Enactus SFU sends a small team to Western Canada Regionals, where the ventures the club runs throughout the year, including Unify, get presented on stage in front of a room of judges from companies like RBC. Presenting isn't reserved for the people who built the venture. The club opens it up to anyone willing to put in the work, and this year that meant about 60 people applied for 8 spots.

I'd spent most of the year on Unify's strategy and partnerships side: outreach emails, research, the kind of work that happens behind a venture rather than in front of an audience. When the call for presenters went out, the first thing I thought wasn't really about myself. Unify had become something I genuinely cared about, and I wanted it represented well on a stage I wasn't going to be on otherwise. The second thing, closer to the real reason, was that I'd never done anything like this and wanted to find out what it would take to get good at it.

I didn't have a strong case for why it should be me. I'd done class presentations and the occasional pitch, but nothing with judges, a strict clock, and a result the rest of the club would actually feel. When I found out I'd been selected, the feeling wasn't really "I earned this." It was closer to "okay, now I have to figure out how to do this."

Writing something meant to be said out loud

The work split into two halves: what we'd say, and how we'd say it. A dedicated designer handled the visual side, building the slides and materials that would sit behind us on stage. My side was the script and the delivery, and I took ownership of both, from early drafts through to the final run.

There was a part of this I was more worried about than the public speaking itself: compressing a year of Unify's work, work I'd been part of but hadn't built alone, into a few minutes on stage without flattening it into a pitch. Unify is a project a lot of people had put real care into long before Regionals existed, and I didn't want my inexperience up there to be the reason it didn't land.

Writing for the stage turned out to be its own skill, and not one I had yet. My first drafts read like reports: dense, long, and full of phrasing that looks fine on a page and falls apart the moment you try to say it out loud. Coaches pushed on structure first: what to lead with, what to cut, and where the actual story was buried under context nobody needed. A lot of those early sessions weren't about polishing what I had. They were about finding the version of the material that worked as something spoken rather than something written.

Rehearsing until it stopped sounding memorized

Once the script existed, the real work started: saying it, over and over, until it stopped sounding like something I'd memorized and started sounding like something I actually meant.

Some of that was repetition. Some of it was recording run-throughs and watching them back, which is uncomfortable in a specific way: hearing your own filler words and watching your own nervous habits on a screen isn't fun. But it was the fastest way to fix them. As the weeks went on, the feedback got more specific. Early notes were about what I was saying. Later notes were about pacing, where to pause, and where to let a line sit instead of rushing into the next one.

Not every rehearsal felt like progress. Some sessions were genuinely rough, the kind where you walk out feeling like you've gone backwards. What kept it moving was treating each one as information rather than a verdict. If something didn't land, that was just data for the next run.

Regionals

With some of the team, right before we went on

Walking into the venue with the rest of the team made it feel real in a way that rehearsing back home hadn't. Everyone was in the same headspace, going over the same lines one more time even though there wasn't much left to change at that point.

By the time we walked on stage, I'd run through the presentation enough times that it felt less like a performance and more like saying something I already knew how to say. That doesn't mean the nerves were gone. They weren't. But they were nerves about the moment itself, not about whether I'd remember what came next, and that difference mattered more than I expected.

Talking with one of the judges after presenting

The part nobody can fully rehearse you for is what comes after. The judges asked questions the script hadn't covered, and answering those well meant relying on actually understanding Unify, not just the version of it I'd memorized for the stage. The months of rehearsal had built the delivery, but the months of actual work on Unify were what got us through the parts that weren't scripted.

Second place

The team, right after the results were announced

We placed 2nd, contributing to $5,000+ in prize winnings for the club and the best regional result Enactus SFU has had in nearly a decade.

The season, in numbers

60+ applicants for 8 presenter spots
1 of 8 selected to represent Enactus SFU at Regionals
2nd place at Western Canada Regionals
$5,000+ contributed to the club's prize winnings

Best regional result for Enactus SFU in nearly a decade.

For a club that had been working toward a result like this for years, it landed as more than a number. It was confirmation that the work the venture teams put in all year, and the work that went into presenting it, actually came through on stage.

What this season actually taught me

The biggest shift wasn't really about presenting. It was about how I responded to not being good at something yet. Going from zero competition experience to a podium finish in one season only worked because I treated every rough rehearsal, every piece of blunt feedback, and every uncomfortable watch-back of my own delivery as something to learn from rather than something to get defensive about.

I don't think I would have pushed through the rougher rehearsals as easily if this had only been about me. Wanting to represent Unify well gave the whole process a reason that went beyond getting better at public speaking for its own sake, and that made it easier to sit through the uncomfortable parts instead of avoiding them.

That's the part I'm carrying past this season: a willingness to be visibly bad at something in front of people whose feedback I actually want, because that's the fastest way to stop being bad at it.
